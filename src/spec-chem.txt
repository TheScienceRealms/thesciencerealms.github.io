Assessment Unit AS 1			 Basic Concepts in Physical
 		 and Inorganic Chemistry
Summary Points
• Candidates should expect practical chemistry to be tested in all units. In this paper,
 tests involving halide ions, hydrogen chloride gas, iodine in solution and titration
 technique required precise wording and observations.
• The Quality of Written Communication question required a coherent description of
 the mass spectrum of chlorine gas. Candidates needed to give the number of peaks,
 the m/z values and the species responsible for the peaks.
• Half-equations, oxidation states and systematic names of inorganic ions remain
 discriminating areas. Candidates should practise balancing half-equations in acidic
 solution and naming ions using correct oxidation-state notation.
• Calculations were accessible where candidates set out their working clearly, used the
 stoichiometry in the equation and gave the final answer to the requested precision.
• Many candidates demonstrated good knowledge of shapes of ions, intermolecular
 forces, ionic bonding and giant structures, but marks were lost where charges, lone
 pairs or the phrase “between molecules” were omitted.
Q1–10 The multiple-choice questions assessed a broad range of AS1 content including
 electron counting, amount of substance, oxidation states, redox, molecular
 polarity, bond angles, melting points, Group VII trends and acid-base titrations.
 The questions requiring direct recall were generally more accessible, such as
 Questions 5, 7, 8, 9 and 10. The more demanding items required candidates
 to combine chemical understanding with calculation, such as 2 and 4 or
 comparison, such as in questions in Questions 1 and 6. In general, the multiple-
 choice questions were well answered.
Q11 This question focused on simple ions, molecular ions, oxidation states and half-
 equations. In Part (a)(i), many candidates were able to identify the correct ions from
 atomic number and electron number, although errors occurred where candidates
 omitted ionic charges or did not use the charge. In Part (a)(ii) the electronic
 configurations were generally well attempted, but candidates should take care to
 write configurations in the correct order and to distinguish between the configuration
 of an ion and that of the corresponding atom. In Part (a)(iii), where a name is asked
 for, the symbol will be penalised. In Part (a)(iv), the majority of candidates were
 able to answer this successfully, but a common error was to use the letters C and D
 in place of Y and Br. In Part (b)(i), the definition of a molecular ion required the idea
 of a group of atoms or two or more atoms with an overall charge; answers which
 described only a charged atom did not gain credit. In Part (b)(ii), candidates needed
 to identify nitrogen as the element oxidised and support this with correct oxidation
 states along with the correct definition of oxidation in terms of oxidation states; some
 candidates incorrectly referenced oxidation in terms of loss of electrons. The half-
 equation for the reduction of manganate(VII) ions was a good discriminator, in
 Part (b)(iii), with common errors including missing H⁺ ions, missing water or an
 unbalanced charge. In Part (b)(iv), systematic names required correct use of oxidation
 states, i.e. manganate(VII) and manganese(II), this was poorly answered, with many
 candidates referring to permanganate.
Q12 This question examined Group VII chemistry, redox reactions, mass spectrometry,
 sterilisation of drinking water and first ionisation energy. In Part (a), equations for
 the reactions of chlorine with sodium hydroxide solution were often attempted, but
 marks were lost through incorrect products or balancing. Most candidates were
 able to successfully distinguish between the products of the reaction of hot and
 concentrated sodium hydroxide solution in comparison to cold and dilute sodium
 hydroxide solution as NaClO but should take care to give the correct order of the
 elements in this compound in Question 12(a)(ii). Many candidates lost the mark in
 Question 12(a)(iii) as they simply gave the definition of disproportionation but did
 not reference the element chlorine. In Part (b), the colours of solid sodium halides
 and the corresponding silver halide precipitates required careful completion of
 the table, many candidates were not credited with the mark for the colour of the
 solid sodium halides but were more successful with the colour of the solid silver
 halides. In Part (c)(i), observations for the reaction of concentrated sulfuric acid
 with solid potassium iodide were usually better known than the formulae and state
 symbols of the products responsible. Some candidates lost marks for not including
 the state symbols despite having the correct product formula. In Part (c)(iii), the
 equation for the reaction of hydrogen iodide with concentrated sulfuric acid was
 more demanding and required correct products and balancing, however, it was well
 attempted and some candidates were able to obtain both marks here. In Part (d),
 stronger responses to the Quality of Written Communication question described
 five peaks in the mass spectrum of chlorine gas at m/z 35, 37, 70, 72 and 74 and
 linked these to the appropriate ions. Weaker responses listed only molecular ion
 peaks or did not identify the species responsible; some candidates could identify
 the species responsible but then did not say what the m/z values were. Many
 candidates did not give the charge on the species and whilst we allowed this mark
 on this occasion, the charges will be required going forward. More care should be
 taken to read the question fully for a Quality of Written Communication question.
 In Part (e), candidates generally knew that ozone is O3 and could give an advantage
 or disadvantage of using ozone rather than chlorine, for Part (i) and (ii). The first
 ionisation energy equation for Part (e)(iii) was also well answered, although common
 errors included incorrect state symbols or incorrect charge of the oxygen. In Part (e)
 (iv) the comparison of oxygen and sulfur required reference to the smaller atomic
 radius of oxygen and the greater attraction between the nucleus and the outer
 electron, if the candidate only referenced sulfur in their answer, they did not gain any
 credit as the question asked for an explanation for oxygen.
Q13 This question assessed bonding and structure, intermolecular forces, hydrogen
 bonding, Group VII boiling points and iodine chemistry. In Part (a)(i), molecule A was
 ethanoic acid, which was well answered and in Part (a)(ii) bond C was a hydrogen
 bond; some candidates gave a covalent bond or simply stated “intermolecular
 force”, which was not specific enough. In Part (b)(i), the dot and cross diagram for
 H3O⁺ required three bonding pairs of electrons, one lone pair of electrons and an
 overall positive charge, this was well answered. In Part (b)(ii), the bond formed
 between water and a hydrogen ion was a co-ordinate bond, dative on its own was
 not accepted, however, dative covalent was. In Part (b)(iii), the shape and bond angle
 were generally well answered where candidates recognised the trigonal pyramidal
 arrangement, however, some candidates incorrectly gave 104° or 109.5° or missed
 the degree sign, which were common errors. In Part (c)(i), explanations of boiling
 point trends needed to refer to stronger van der Waals’ forces between molecules
 down the group and to the increased number of electrons; increasing mass was not
 an acceptable answer. In Part (c)(ii), the higher boiling point of hydrogen fluoride
 compared with that of hydrogen chloride required reference to hydrogen bonding
 between molecules. Candidates should be reminded that “between molecules”
 is essential in these explanations and the intermolecular forces of both molecules
 should be given also. In Part (c)(iii), the test for hydrogen chloride gas was well
 answered but many referred to blue litmus paper changing to red. Reference must be
 made to the glass rod or stopper, the ammonia solution must be concentrated, and
 misty fumes was not credited. A second incorrect test, given with a correct answer,
 results in a candidate losing all marks. The equation involving hydrogen bromide
 and concentrated phosphoric acid required the correct formulae and balancing and
 was well answered in Part (c)(iv). In Part (d), candidates needed to identify iodine as
 having a molecular covalent structure, which some candidates incorrectly answered
 as simple molecular, which is not accepted. In Part (d)(ii), the explanation of solubility
 was poor, with many candidates discussing inability to form hydrogen bonds instead
 of simply stating that it was non-polar and water is polar. In Part (d)(iii), most
 candidates gave starch solution with a blue-black colour as the test for iodine. The
 high melting point of potassium iodide required reference to strong electrostatic
 attractions or strong ionic bonds between oppositely charged ions in a giant ionic
 lattice, then a reference to the energy required to break these. Common errors
 included mentioning molecules or van der Waals’ forces which meant a loss of both
 marks.
Q14 This question focused on weak bases, carbonate ions in water and titration
 calculations involving hydrated sodium carbonate. In Part (a)(i), the definition of
 a weak base required the idea that it only partially dissociates in water, and some
 candidates incorrectly made reference to pH. The ionic equation for carbonate
 ions reacting with water was very poorly answered; candidates needed to show the
 formation of hydrogencarbonate ions and hydroxide ions in Part (a)(ii). The titration
 table in Part (b)(i) required candidates to use the information that the burette was not
 refilled and was well answered. In Part (b)(ii), methyl orange was a suitable indicator
 for this strong acid/carbonate titration, with an appropriate yellow to red colour
 change at the end point was also well attempted. The calculations in
 Part (b)(iii) and (iv) were generally accessible, where candidates used the 2:1 ratio
 between hydrochloric acid and sodium carbonate, scaled from 25.0 cm3 to 250 cm3
 and calculated the relative formula mass before finding x. Common errors included
 using the titre in cm3 without converting to dm3, overlooking the 2:1 stoichiometry or
 giving an answer to an inappropriate precision. Error carried forward was awarded
 where appropriate for Part (b)(iv).
Q15 This question assessed metallic bonding, ionic bonding, peroxide ions, equations and
 the bonding and structure of graphite. In Part (a), the description of the bonding in
 sodium was well answered and again reference needed to be made to the attraction
 between the cations and delocalised electrons. In Part (b), dot and cross diagrams for
 sodium oxide were generally well attempted, but some candidates omitted charges,
 showed covalent bonding or included inner shell electrons. In Part (c), candidates
 needed to write a balanced equation for the conversion of sodium oxide to sodium
 peroxide; this was well attempted and multiples were often given. The octet rule
 in Part (d)(i) required the idea of atoms having eight electrons in their outer shell,
 however, many candidates forgot to reference the outer shell. The peroxide ion
 diagram in Part (d)(ii) was more challenging because candidates had to show an O–O
 covalent bond, the correct number of outer electrons and the overall 2- charge. In
 Part (d)(iii), candidates answered well, where the reaction of sodium peroxide with
 water required sodium hydroxide and oxygen as products and correct balancing.
 In Part (e)(i), many candidates could not give a diagram to show the bonding and
 structure in graphite. Three hexagons which tessellate in at least two layers was the
 minimum required standard, prior to labelling. Atom on its own was not accepted
 and a reference to carbon atom or atoms was needed. In Part (e)(ii), the explanation
 of electrical conductivity required reference to delocalised electrons which are free to
 move and carry charge, as usual references to current or electricity were not credited
 and free does not equate to delocalised.
Assessment Unit AS 2			 Further Physical and
 		 Inorganic Chemistry and
 		 an Introduction to Organic
 		 Chemistry
Summary Points
• The overall performance across the cohort showed a reasonable understanding of the
 specification content, with strengths observed in calculating empirical and molecular
 formulae and understanding the effect of changing conditions on the position of
 equilibrium.
• There was also a noticeable improvement in knowledge of definitions as given in the
 Clarification of Terms document.
• However, it is apparent that chemical connectivity, mechanisms and relevant areas of
 practical Chemistry posed the most significant challenge in this unit this year.
• Candidates are reminded that clarity and accuracy in chemical formulae, mechanisms
 and naming are essential to achieve higher marks, and incorrect or ambiguous
 representations will continue to be penalised.
• Candidates are increasingly cognisant of giving answers to the appropriate number of
 significant figures or decimal places as indicated in the question. Calculations were
 generally answered well with most candidates accessing some marks. Candidates
 who set out their work clearly were able to gain partial credit in calculation questions.
Q 1–10 The multiple-choice questions were generally very well answered.
 Questions 1, 5 and 6 were the best answered and Question 3 was the most
 challenging question.
Q11 This question assessed E/Z isomerism in alkenes and was generally poorly answered.
 In Part (a), most candidates scored one mark for referring to restricted rotation
 around C=C but most did not fully explain the reason to achieve the second mark for
 the given alkene. Candidates were required to state that there were different atoms
 or groups connected to each carbon atom in the C=C and in most cases, the term
 atoms or groups was missing. There were some issues with connectivity in Part (b)
 and only the highest achieving candidates gave a fully comparative answer explaining
 the relative priorities of the atoms and groups attached to each carbon in the C=C.
 Many candidates correctly explained that the Z isomer had both of the higher priority
 groups on the same side of the C=C. Most candidates did not fully read the question
 in Part (c) and gave the name and structure of an unbranched isomer, commonly
 2-chlorobut-1-ene.
Q12 Candidates were asked to calculate empirical and molecular formulae in Part (a)
 and Part (b) and most candidates achieved full marks. Part (c) proved to be more
 challenging, particularly the structure of the dinitrile. Many candidates correctly
 identified the alkene in Part (d).
Q13 This question assessed aspects of changing conditions on the position of equilibrium
 as well as alkane chemistry and was generally well answered. In Part (a)(i), most
 candidates were able to fully explain the decrease in yield of propene with an increase
 in pressure; similarly, the majority of candidates were able to deduce that the forward
 reaction was endothermic in Part (a)(ii) and explain why. Many candidates were
 able to explain how a catalyst increases the rate of reaction in Part (a)(iii), however,
 it was noted that many answered the question by stating that a catalyst lowers
 the activation energy, which was not credited for the first mark. The equilibrium
 constant expression was poorly answered in Part (a)(iv), with many candidates not
 writing an equation in the form Kc = or not using square brackets around each term.
 Part (b) assessed radical substitution, and most candidates correctly completed
 the mechanism in Part (b)(i). The name of the mechanism, the termination step
 and product formed by termination were generally well known. A range of correct
 answers were accepted for the possible dichlorinated product in Part (b)(v).
Q14 The definition of structural isomers was well known in Part (a), as was the structure
 and name of the alcohol in Part (b). The definition of a nucleophile was well known in
 Part (c)(i), with most candidates scoring at least one mark. Some candidates did not
 reference an ion or molecule, or the lone pair of electrons, and so did not score the
 first mark. The type of reaction between the 1-chlorobutane and sodium hydroxide
 was very well known, and almost all candidates correctly identified the polarity of
 the C–Cl bond. As with previous series, the mechanism in Part (c)(iv) discriminated
 between candidates. Common errors included curly arrows not starting on a bond
 or from a lone pair of electrons, the carbocation intermediate structure not being
 included, and the final Cl- missing.
Q15 Part (a) was poorly answered which was surprising. Many candidates calculated the
 heat evolved but did not continue to calculate the number of moles of hexane and
 enthalpy change. Some candidates did not include a negative sign with their final
 answer. Whilst many candidates correctly identified heat loss as a factor in
 Part (b), most were unable to correctly identify incomplete combustion of hexane as
 the other main reason why the value calculated was different to the data book value.
 Candidates had difficulty expressing their answer to this part or referred to average
 bond enthalpies. Despite being given the balanced symbol equation, many candidates
 had difficulty calculating the energy required to break bonds or the energy released
 when bonds form in Part (c). Whilst errors were carried forward to the final part of
 the calculation, many did not divide by two to give the molar enthalpy change. The
 definitions of the standard enthalpy of formation and Hess’s Law were well known
 in Part (d)(i). Most candidates were able to write the equation representing the
 standard enthalpy of formation of propane in Part (d)(iii) and correctly calculated the
 value for propane in Part (d)(iv).
Q16 This question assessed aspects of Group II Chemistry and proved to discriminate
 effectively between candidates. Whilst most candidates achieved full marks for
 the equation in Part (a)(i), most candidates had difficulty explaining the lack of a
 precipitate in Part (a)(ii). Some candidates did refer to solubility but were not specific
 enough and did not relate the concept to Group II hydroxides. In Part (b), candidates
 were asked to apply knowledge to Strontium and most found it challenging. Most
 candidates correctly gave the equation for the reaction between strontium and
 water in Part (b)(i), however very few achieved full marks in Part (b)(ii), with many
 stating incorrectly that the solid disappears. As noted in previous reports, it is
 expected that electronic configurations are given in principal quantum number order
 and consequently a significant number of candidates did not get the mark for the
 configuration of strontium in Part (b)(iii). The comparison in reactivity between
 calcium and strontium was very poorly answered in Part (b)(iv). Whilst many correctly
 identified the increase in shielding as the main reason, candidates did not go on and
 explain the relative ease in removing outer electrons. Many referred to the outer
 electron, rather than electrons, in strontium which was not credited. The oxidation
 half-equation in Part (b)(v) was also poorly answered, with many candidates including
 water in the equation.
Q17 The majority of candidates correctly identified acidified potassium dichromate(VI)
 as the oxidising agent in Part (a). The Quality of Written Communication question
 discriminated effectively between candidates. Most were able to state the names of
 the two oxidation products as well as reference reflux and distillation as the relevant
 experimental methods. Only the highest achieving candidates scored full marks
 as candidates were expected to reference the C=O and O–H bonds in propan-1-ol,
 propanal and propanoic acid. Many did not reference the lack of a C=O absorption
 in propan-1-ol or the lack of an O–H absorption in propanal and so did not access
 the higher band marks. The diagram illustrating hydrogen bonding between propan-
 1-ol and water was poorly answered in Part (c), with many candidates omitting
 relevant lone pairs of electrons or not labelling the hydrogen bond. In Part (d), most
 candidates correctly explained why 2-methylpropan-2-ol cannot be oxidised in
 Part (d)(i) and received at least partial credit for naming and giving the structures
 of other isomers which were also tertiary alcohols and could not be oxidised.
 Connectivity between carbon and oxygen in the C–OH was poor in some structures,
 with many drawing the bond from the carbon atom to the gap between the oxygen
 and hydrogen atoms in the functional group. Similar poor connectivity was noted
 when a bond was drawn between a carbon atom and a methyl group, with the bond
 often being drawn to the hydrogen atoms in the –CH3 group.
